Good morning. This is your AI news digest for Wednesday, November fourth. Today's episode draws on The Rundown, TLDR AI, and AI Breakfast. Apple opens its new Siri to developers, Anthropic's prospectus gets a closer read, and OpenAI recruits the hackers who broke into its biggest rival. Let's get into it.

We start with Apple, the one story all three newsletters led with. Apple released a developer preview of Siri A.I. in its latest software betas, including the i.O.S. twenty-seven point three beta, giving apps of any kind a way to build their own features into the rebuilt assistant. Siri A.I. can see a user's screen and read messages, and it has been a waitlisted beta since i.O.S. twenty-seven shipped in September. Until now, developers were limited to Apple's preset list of actions. Now they can add their own, and teach Siri multi-step jobs through new "skills," exposing their app's content and actions for multi-step requests. The catch is timing. For now, those app versions stay with each developer's internal testers, App Store submissions don't start until spring, and Apple says the rules for developers will come later. TLDR AI notes Siri A.I. is currently unavailable in the E.U. and for users under thirteen. The same betas bring China its first Apple Intelligence tools, minus Siri A.I., and test Apple Watch features that listen in the background to transcribe nearby conversation. The Rundown called it a real crack in Apple's walled garden, but said the best A.I. features still feel a long way off for the world's most popular phone. Separately, AI Breakfast flags that Apple's annual filing warns A.I. agents may behave in "unintended or unpredictable" ways.

Next, a closer look at Anthropic's amended I.P.O. filing, which we touched on yesterday. AI Breakfast dug into the numbers. Third-quarter revenue was about seventeen billion dollars, up more than eleven hundred percent from a year earlier and about forty-seven percent from the second quarter. The company swung to an operating loss of five hundred sixty-seven million dollars, which one analysis of the filing attributes mostly to five billion dollars of compute bought under short-term commitments that Anthropic says currently costs significantly more, plus a nearly two billion dollar non-cash charge tied to donation matching. The headline net loss of about twenty-three billion dollars is almost entirely a non-cash accounting loss on convertible notes and similar instruments, and operating cash flow over nine months was positive, at about six and a half billion dollars. The bigger number is commitments: about eight hundred ninety-five billion dollars in purchase commitments and leases, nearly all of it compute and infrastructure, with roughly seventy billion due in the next twelve months. The Google Cloud commitment alone rose to about one hundred twenty-one billion dollars. And in the risk factors, Anthropic says it "may imminently meet" its Responsible Scaling Policy definition of dramatically accelerated progress attributable to A.I., while stating it has not designated any current model as exceeding that threshold, and that it is weighing policy updates to keep humans in control of frontier model development. AI Breakfast's take: writing that in a document with legal consequences is a different act than writing it in a research post. In related news, Business Insider reports Claude Code's product leader Cat Wu has left Anthropic weeks before the I.P.O.

That brings us to OpenAI and security. OpenAI says it is partnering with a firm called Cathedral for its next internal security sprint, with Cathedral's researchers using OpenAI's models to challenge its defenses. Cathedral was founded by four former DOGE staffers and launched earlier this year at a reported one point four billion dollar valuation. Last week it said it got into Anthropic's internal systems through Anthropic's own bug bounty program, surfacing Slack messages and meeting transcripts. Anthropic said sensitive systems like model weights and Claude conversations were untouched, and called the approach unusual and out of scope. OpenAI also repriced its bug bounty: a critical finding in ChatGPT, Codex, or the A.P.I. now pays a base of up to thirty thousand dollars, with bonuses pushing a single award as high as about one hundred seventy-two thousand. Meanwhile, Reuters reported, citing five sources, that OpenAI employees raised security concerns that reached senior leaders years before ChatGPT launched. OpenAI disputes that it neglected security.

Staying with A.I. and security, CrowdStrike unveiled what it calls the A.I. Kill Switch, an emergency stop in its Falcon Guardian platform for businesses worried about their A.I. agents going rogue. It cuts an agent's link to its model rather than touching the model itself, a human admin has to pull the trigger, and every use is logged. C.E.O. George Kurtz compared it to a race car's brake, saying it sits outside the A.I. so an agent "can't argue with it, rewrite it, or wait it out." CrowdStrike is also buying monitoring firm Fiddler A.I. and security tester Dreadnode for a reported four hundred ninety-five million dollars.

Now to Google and schools. Google announced that starting early next year, K through twelve student accounts will begin with Search, Gemini, and Gemini Notebook all switched off, which could force districts that want A.I. out of classrooms to give up Google Search too. Google's education vice president told The New York Times that "Search and A.I. are deeply integrated." The move comes a day after a deadline senators gave Google to answer questions about pushing A.I. into schools, and former F.T.C. Chair Lina Khan accused Google of using its search monopoly to coerce schools. Google defended it as more choice, not less.

A few numbers from OpenAI, via an interview covered by TLDR AI and AI Breakfast. ChatGPT's search-like volume is now about twenty percent of Google's. ChatGPT reportedly added two hundred million users in the past two months, reaching one point two billion weekly users, and voice mode now has about two hundred fifty million weekly users. OpenAI also plans to merge ChatGPT's Chat and Work modes, and GIF output is coming to its image generation. Separately, OpenAI's new LifeVal benchmark puts G.P.T. six Astra at about eighty-one percent on everyday tasks, up from about forty-nine percent for G.P.T. five point two.

One follow-up on yesterday's M.I.T. research, where G.P.T. six Astra edited a small Qwen model's weights and lifted its math score from about eight percent to forty-eight. AI Breakfast adds the caveats: it took fifty attempts, results barely moved at ten or twenty-five, the method did nothing for Google's tiny Gemma model, and networks written from scratch beat conventional training in only one of three simple games. TLDR AI pointed to a blog post calling the "Astra can read and write weights" claims misleading.

In chips, DeepSeek reportedly plans to pretrain its next-generation model, DeepSeek V five, on Huawei's Ascend chips, which have mostly been used for inference. A successful run could reduce Chinese labs' dependence on Nvidia. And Nvidia reportedly told Asian cloud customers that fifty-one percent of their services must go to non-Chinese firms or they lose chip access.

Some quick hits. Anthropic and university researchers released FlashProve, an open-source way to cryptographically prove which model produced an answer. A.M.D.'s data center revenue nearly doubled to a record eight point five billion dollars. Super Micro's quarterly revenue hit almost twenty billion dollars, up nearly three hundred percent. Oracle filed to sell twenty billion dollars in stock and bonds to fund its A.I. buildout. Fal, the A.I. image and video hosting platform, raised one point three billion dollars at a sixteen billion dollar valuation. Revolut says self-hosting G.L.M. on Nvidia chips cut its A.I. coding costs by about ninety percent. Goldman Sachs pulled advanced A.I. coding tools from traders and bankers, keeping them with engineers. The New York Times and other publishers sued Brave over alleged scraping. Postman's desktop app reportedly installed plugins and a startup hook into Codex and Claude Code without asking. OpenAI hired former Army Secretary Dan Driscoll as a national security counselor. Netflix is testing an A.I. Discover tab that recommends shows by mood. And at SETI's Allen Telescope Array, A.I. agents now run operations, and detected a Crab Nebula pulsar in real time.

That's the digest for today.
