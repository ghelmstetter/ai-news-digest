Good morning. This is your A.I. news digest for Friday, November thirteenth. Today's episode draws on The Rundown, TLDR A.I., A.I. Breakfast, and Built In. The big story: Jensen Huang has resigned as Nvidia's C.E.O. After that, Google's new Gemini four Hydrogen model, a lawsuit asking a California court to freeze frontier A.I. development, and a forensic claim that top Chinese labs are training on export-controlled Nvidia chips.

We start with Nvidia. On Thursday, Nvidia said Jensen Huang resigned as president, C.E.O., and board member, effective immediately, ending a thirty-three year run at the company he co-founded. Chief financial officer Colette Kress, in that role since 2013, becomes interim C.E.O. while keeping the C.F.O. job. Harvey C. Jones, a director since 1993 and a former C.E.O. of Synopsys, was named the company's first-ever board chair. The search for a permanent C.E.O. covers both internal and external candidates.

The exit came days after Huang admitted to an extramarital affair, following reports linking him to a woman U.S. intelligence officials reportedly assessed to be a Chinese agent. At the time, the board backed him, saying it had no reason to believe national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised, and A.I. Breakfast notes none of the intelligence allegations have been proven. Pressure built after The New York Times reported a second relationship, with Nvidia health care vice president Kimberly Powell. The Rundown adds that Reuters reported a third relationship, with another employee, after the resignation. The Rundown also says President Trump pitched Tim Cook as the "only one man" who could take the job, and that House committee chairs are seeking a closed-door briefing from Huang.

On the market side, Nvidia stock closed down about four percent Thursday after falling more than fourteen percent during the day. A.I. Breakfast points out that A.M.D. rose almost six percent and Broadcom five and a half, which it reads as the market pricing in a weaker Nvidia rather than a weaker A.I. trade. Kress inherits a business that did ninety-six billion dollars in revenue in the first half of fiscal 2027, up eighty-five percent year over year, and she reports third-quarter results on Tuesday, November seventeenth. A few related threads: Semafor argues Huang's exit ends the so-called "Nvidia put" that has been backstopping the A.I. trade, and Hugging Face says Nvidia's twelve point nine billion dollar acquisition of it is still expected to close in 2027.

Staying with Nvidia, there is a separate story about its chips in China. A forensic analysis of publicly released models found evidence that DeepSeek, Moonshot A.I., MiniMax, and Ant Group have gained access to Nvidia's export-controlled Blackwell G.P.U.s. According to TLDR A.I., six models these companies released since April contain numerical patterns that match Blackwell hardware. The Rundown notes former OpenAI researcher Will DePue made the claim publicly. The analysis is careful about its limits, though: it doesn't establish where the chips were, how access was obtained, or whether export controls were actually violated.

Next, Google. Google shipped Gemini four Hydrogen, which it calls the daily driver for agentic workflows: a fast, efficient model optimized for coding and agents, leaving Gemini four Carbon and Argon to handle deep reasoning. All three newsletters covered it. Hydrogen has four thinking levels, including a new extra-high tier, a one million token input window, and up to one million output tokens. In Google's own reported figures, it scores fifty-one on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at high thinking, above Argon's fifty, and fifty-four at extra-high. On Terminal-Bench it hits fifty-seven percent, against about eighteen percent for Gemini three point eight Flash, and it finishes tasks roughly two and a half times faster. Pricing is a dollar fifty per million input tokens and seven fifty per million output tokens, flat across the whole context window. It's rolling out across the Gemini A.P.I., A.I. Studio, Antigravity, the Gemini app, and Search.

Google also shipped the Gemini Decisions A.P.I. Instead of generating text, it scores one input against up to a thousand yes-or-no, multiple-choice, or rubric questions in a single pass, returning a confidence for each, in roughly a hundred fifty to two hundred fifty milliseconds. It costs ten cents per million input tokens, with output free. A.I. Breakfast calls it the most practical thing Google has released this month.

And Google opened pre-orders for its first Gemini-powered smart glasses, built with Samsung, with frames from Warby Parker and Gentle Monster starting at three hundred ninety-five dollars. There's no display. They have a twelve megapixel camera, open-ear speakers, and up to nine hours of battery. You can say "Hey Gemini" to ask about what you're looking at, get directions, or translate speech live across more than seventy languages. Google says the glasses redact bystanders' faces before Gemini sees them and stop recording if the recording light is covered. They go on sale December tenth in the U.S. and South Korea, and work with iPhones. The Rundown frames it as a direct challenge to Meta, which shipped eighty-one percent of A.I. glasses in the first half of the year but has struggled with privacy backlash.

Now to the courts. A coalition of four nonprofits, including Public Citizen and the Heat Initiative, sued in San Francisco on Thursday, naming OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and SpaceX's A.I. unit. They want an order blocking development of models more capable than what these companies already ship, arguing unfair business practices and public nuisance. A.I. Breakfast calls it a long shot, but notes the complaint leans on the labs' own published warnings, including Anthropic's prospectus saying it "may imminently meet" an acceleration threshold in its Responsible Scaling Policy. Relatedly, Democratic lawmakers have reportedly drafted a bill requiring a federal permit to train advanced A.I. models.
