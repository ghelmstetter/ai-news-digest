Next, science. Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome two, and both newsletters cover it. The model predicts how small differences in DNA between people play out inside specific cell types. The original version averaged across whole tissues; version two looks at each cell type individually. DeepMind's Adam Gayoso called it tying human genetics to cell biology.

The numbers: in a test across twenty-eight immune cell types, the original model caught just sixteen percent of the DNA changes behind shifts in gene activity. AlphaGenome two nearly doubles that, to thirty percent. Labs can also hand it gene activity from just a few dozen cells, such as immune cells activated by a vaccine, and get predictions without retraining the model. Tested on inflammatory bowel disease, it matched a recent study by picking out a DNA change that overworks one gene in the gut's immune cells. It's available for non-commercial use. The pitch is that scientists have found many DNA quirks tied to diseases like type two diabetes, but often can't tell which cells they actually affect, which is the part drug makers need.
