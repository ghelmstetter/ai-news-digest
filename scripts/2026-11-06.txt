Good morning. This is your A.I. news digest for Friday, November sixth. Today's episode draws on The Rundown, TLDR A.I., A.I. Breakfast, and Built In. Three big threads today: an outside look at how fast Anthropic is moving internally, OpenAI's most dangerous cyber model yet, and a rough day on Wall Street after voters turned against data centers.

We start with Anthropic. The A.I. evaluation nonprofit METR has published a report based on eleven weeks of inside access to Anthropic's internal data and interviews with staff. Its conclusion: Anthropic's A.I. progress is likely running about twice as fast as its pace in 2024 and 2025, on well-defined tasks. METR's estimated range runs from one point three to three times faster, and it credits A.I. itself for around eighty percent of that speedup. METR also says it found no clear bottlenecks that would slow the loop down.

There's a catch. Anthropic redacted most of the strongest evidence. Co-founder and safety lead Sam McCandlish said the redactions were meant to avoid, in his words, "handing out a roadmap for others to go faster." The report does reveal two internal models ahead of Anthropic's top public model, Claude Opus five point five, including one called "Model 4" that cleared fifty percent on all fifteen benchmarks tested. The Rundown notes that Anthropic's own I.P.O. filing defines a danger threshold as "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to A.I.," and that Monday's update said that point may be close. METR's Ryan Greenblatt said he was "seriously wrong" after expecting a much slower speedup this year. And A.I. Breakfast passes along rumors of a big Anthropic announcement next week, while The Rundown says the next major model release is widely expected this week.

A few smaller Anthropic items. Claude now has a Personal Finance feature that lets users securely connect bank and credit card accounts and ask about balances, transactions, and spending. For enterprises, Anthropic made prompt and pre-tool-call hooks generally available, which route prompts and proposed tool calls through a company's existing data loss prevention vendor before they execute. A security center and a cross-organization parent console are in beta. Claude Code background agents now write their status updates with Haiku five point five instead of the session model, which Anthropic says cuts the cost per update by about ninety-nine percent. Anthropic also signed its first licensing deal with a digital publisher, putting attributed A.P. election data into Claude.

Now to OpenAI, which released G.P.T. six Cyber, a model built to find and exploit software vulnerabilities. OpenAI rated it Critical under its Preparedness Framework, the top cyber tier, and a notch above its predecessor. On OpenAI's own ExploitGym evaluation it scored forty-eight point five percent, versus thirty-two point one for the previous version, and it beats the main G.P.T. six Astra model on most cyber tasks. OpenAI says it surfaced eighty-one previously unknown high-severity bugs in Firefox, and The Register reports Mozilla has started shipping fixes.

A companion writeup describes an October campaign against the Linux kernel. The model analyzed about twenty-three hundred candidate findings, reproduced more than seventeen hundred of them within thirty hours, and ended with nine hundred forty-eight working privilege escalation exploits, five hundred eighty-eight of them previously unknown, along with patches. The total bill was about one point two million dollars in A.P.I. credits, or roughly twelve hundred eighty dollars per exploit. OpenAI says the model stayed inside its authorized boundaries more than ninety-nine percent of the time, with seven hundredths of a percent of cases going out of scope, and it declined to say what happened in those. A.I. Breakfast points out that G.P.T. six point one Astra was shelved in September for overstepping authorized boundaries. Access is limited to vetted Daybreak Red customers for now, with a wider rollout promised in the coming weeks.

OpenAI also launched Images two point six across ChatGPT and Codex. It's fifty percent faster than the last version, keeps reference images persistent, and for the first time adds motion: it can turn a prompt, photo, or sketch into an animated GIF, and users can change the speed or edit individual frames. Developers get two A.P.I. versions, Sunburst for higher quality and Flare for faster, lighter jobs.

Next, the money side of the midterms. We mentioned yesterday that voters were turning against data centers. The fuller tally from A.I. Breakfast: data centers were on the ballot forty-one times across ten states, and voters backed a restriction or an outright ban in all but one. Ohio carried twenty-one of those measures, with several towns capping data centers at twenty-five megawatts and others banning them outright. All seven Illinois measures went against development, and a Wisconsin measure now requires a public vote on any development over four hundred fifty million dollars.

Markets reacted Thursday. The Nasdaq fell five point five two percent, its worst day since April 2025 and into correction territory. The S&P five hundred dropped four point three six percent, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost more than twelve percent. Nvidia closed down more than nine percent, A.M.D. fell more than seventeen percent, Broadcom about ten, and the so-called neocloud names, IREN, Applied Digital, and Nebius, each lost more than fourteen percent. One analyst at eToro summed it up: the market now expects the buildout to be "slower and more expensive." Related items: Microsoft declined an option to more than double its Texas capacity deal with Nscale, which is planning a thirty-five billion dollar I.P.O., and Apple is reportedly cutting employee A.I. budgets, with some daily allowances dropping from three hundred to two hundred dollars. On the politics side, Arizona Representative Yassamin Ansari said a pause on advanced A.I. and a ban on superintelligence should be at the top of the Democratic agenda on day one of the new House majority.

Following up on this week's coding deals. Beyond OpenAI's purchase of Pierre and Meta's purchase of Conductor, which we covered yesterday, A.I. Breakfast adds that Axios framed the Pierre deal as a run at GitHub, and that Conductor's cloud service shuts down November twentieth, with user cloud data deleted after that. Also, Cat Wu, who led Claude Code product and left Anthropic on October thirtieth, has joined OpenAI to run product management for Core Products.

Now a quick round of model and tool releases. Thinking Machines introduced Inkling two Small, a mixture-of-experts model with five hundred ninety-four billion total parameters and seventeen billion active, supporting image inputs. It's available now through Tinker with closed weights, and full weights are due next week under an Apache license after external safety testing. Google's Gemini Omni one point two Flash cuts the price of its video model by thirty percent, to seven cents a second at seven-twenty p, and stretches clips from ten seconds to fifteen. Cursor now lets users describe a tool, then builds it, sets up a database, runs security checks, and deploys it as a shareable live app. Perplexity announced Teammates, agents that each own an ongoing project, and C.E.O. Aravind Srinivas says he anticipates every organization having hundreds of them per person. Notion opened shared memory so ChatGPT, Claude, and other assistants can read the same workspace context.

From research: Axiom released a Lean formalization of the entire Stacks Project, the open reference for modern algebraic geometry, which it calls the largest integrated library of formalized mathematics ever built. Google DeepMind published a minimal open implementation of AlphaEvolve, its Gemini-powered algorithm design agent. Nvidia released Cosmos three, four open-weight world simulation models for robotics and driving, plus VFlash, a vision model that predicts robot trajectories in parallel rather than token by token.

A few closing notes. Uber says just four riders used an A.I. agent to book a trip during its busiest October week.
