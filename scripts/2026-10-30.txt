Good morning. This is your AI news digest for Friday, October thirtieth. Today's episode draws on The Rundown, TLDR AI, and Built In. Six stories today, starting with the biggest.

First up: a spy story around Nvidia. The New York Times reports that U.S. officials concluded Alice Huang, an executive at the Singapore data center firm Megaspeed, was allegedly passing intelligence to Beijing while keeping close ties with Nvidia C.E.O. Jensen Huang. President Trump was reportedly briefed about a year ago. Officials didn't believe Jensen Huang knew about her alleged role, and he never received the defensive briefing usually given to Americans targeted by spies. Megaspeed is already under U.S. investigation for steering Nvidia chips to China. Through a sister company, it bought at least four point eight billion dollars of Nvidia hardware, and it's still an Nvidia cloud partner. Nvidia calls the meetings routine and says nothing relevant to national security was shared. China calls the claims groundless smears. Nothing has been publicly proven. The practical fallout may land on the loophole that lets companies like Megaspeed rent Nvidia-powered computing from Southeast Asia to Chinese A.I. labs.

Next, a cluster of Anthropic news. Built In reports that Anthropic has officially filed to go public, in what could become one of the biggest I.P.O.s in history. Separately, Bloomberg Law reports that Anthropic is now SpaceX's largest single customer. It reportedly accounted for thirty-four and a half percent of SpaceX's third-quarter revenue through A.I. compute contracts, with spending set to reach three point one billion dollars a month starting in December. And on the safety side, Anthropic introduced a technique it calls "self-assessment," in which Claude reviews each conversation after replying and flags potential misuse. Anthropic says it's been running since June. On Claude Fable 5, it catches seventy-eight percent of potentially harmful conversations flagged by a stronger classifier, and ninety-four percent of the high-priority ones, at a one percent false-positive rate. Anthropic is candid that it's weak as a standalone blocker. In red-team tests it cut attack success only from seventy-nine to fifty-four percent, though it made each successful attack cost about four times as much.

Third: open weights at the frontier. Mistral released the weights for Mistral Large 4, a trillion-parameter model built for self-hosted deployment and cybersecurity work. Mistral claims leading open-weight performance on agentic coding benchmarks, and cyber capabilities comparable to G.L.M. five point three. That cyber angle connects to a sobering piece from Epoch A.I. It found that G.L.M. five point three can take security fixes that have been published but not yet shipped to users, and turn them into working exploits for recent versions of Firefox. Widely available open-weight models can now do much, or all, of that work on their own. Open-source projects are especially exposed, because their code is public.

Fourth: Sam Altman's long interview on the Cheeky Pint podcast, which both newsletters picked up. Altman says he'd rather see a trillion slightly superhuman A.I.s join society than one A.I. with a trillion I.Q. His argument is that many agents checking and collaborating with each other look more like a resilient society than one centralized superintelligence. He also said OpenAI will point its robotics program toward data centers, and that self-replicating data centers, with robots running chip fabs and building solar panels, are now within reach. In related OpenAI news, the company updated its Model Spec, organizing model behavior around five values: faithfulness to human intent, harm minimization, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care.

Fifth: agents working together. Researchers at Raindrop put eight agents from four different models together to resolve simulated outages, with no designated leader. Two things predicted which agent took charge: the underlying model, and, surprisingly, the order the agents' names appeared in the prompt. The takeaway is that small configuration details can shape how an agent swarm coordinates. On the engineering side, Factory released Droid one point oh, an agent harness that can run hundreds of tool calls in a single model request, tuned across many models so improvements generalize. And Meta described how it scaled its L.L.M.-powered "just-in-time" testing system into production to catch bugs before code lands.

Finally, quick hits. Cohere announced a Series E targeting up to five billion dollars, reporting a hundred sixty-five percent year-over-year growth in annual recurring revenue. Harvey acquired Aida, an A.I. sales startup. Meta launched Style Sketch, an A.I. fashion app that builds a virtual wardrobe and generates try-ons. Canva introduced Brush, a model that edits designs from text. Databricks launched managed long-term memory for agents. Pika released H3 Flex for video generation, working from up to twelve references and outputting up to 4K. Fish Audio's Drama 3 now ranks first among open-weight models on both Artificial Analysis speech arenas. Figma explained how it trained a specialized model for editing in Figma. And Google DeepMind published an essay on the economics of intelligence, arguing that A.I. is improving fast on individual tasks but lags on coordination, and that wages and job roles will be the earliest signals of real economic change.

One last thought, from Rowan Cheung at The Rundown. He argues that "sent from my agent" is becoming the new "sent from my iPhone," and that it tells the recipient they weren't worth your attention. His rule: anything that touches a person, he does himself. Anything that's just a task, an agent can handle.

That's the digest for today. Have a good workout.
