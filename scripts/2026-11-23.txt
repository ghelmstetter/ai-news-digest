Good morning. This is your A.I. news digest for Monday, November twenty-third. Today's episode draws on The Rundown, A.I. Breakfast, and TLDR A.I.

The biggest story of the weekend is mathematical. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, says that working with Claude he has found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. First posed in eighteen fifty-nine, the problem asks whether prime numbers follow a predictable pattern. It is often ranked as math's most famous open problem, and the Clay Institute put a one million dollar Millennium Prize on it in two thousand. According to The Rundown, the work took about six weeks.

Alpöge opened a note on his own site on Friday with a flat line: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." He skipped the traditional paper. Instead he published the proof as a library of Lean code, the computer language used to verify mathematics, running to about seventeen million lines. He writes that the method extends further, to the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and that Claude "flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line." A.I. Breakfast notes he does not say which Claude model he used.

The big question was whether it holds up, and so far the answer is yes. The groups behind Lean and its math library certified it on Saturday, saying they have "full confidence" the proof is correct, that the library compiles, and that the code proves the right statement. Andrew Sutherland of M.I.T. built it on his own machine, says he has seen at least three more checks by professional mathematicians, and is quoted putting the odds that something is wrong at roughly zero.

But nobody fully understands it yet. Kevin Buzzard of Imperial College London said no one yet understands what the proof shows. The Association for Human Mathematics criticized it as "little more than publicity" for A.I. labs. Alpöge has committed to producing a human-readable explanation, and TLDR says the community is expected to help explain the ideas in the coming weeks.

There was a twist the same day. Stanford's Ravi Vakil posted a proposed proof of the generalized hypothesis by Alessandro Maria Masullo, the product of about twelve years of work, with the note "it is all human, with no A.I." But several people soon reported problems in the draft, which, as Vakil noted, were identified with the help of different A.I. models. As A.I. Breakfast put it: the machine-checkable proof holds up and nobody can read it, while the readable one could be read, which is exactly how the holes got found.

The other big story is at Nvidia. Jensen Huang is back. Nvidia's board voted unanimously on Monday to reinstate Huang as president, chief executive, and a board director, effective immediately, eleven days after he resigned. The board says it is "satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead NVIDIA." Harvey C. Jones, who became Nvidia's first-ever board chair when Huang left, stays chair, and Colette Kress hands back the C.E.O. job and continues as C.F.O.

A committee of independent directors is still investigating reports that Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and the board says that investigation will proceed to conclusion with his full support. The board says it received input from senior levels of the United States government and is confident his reinstatement poses no national security risk. Nvidia is also creating a National Security and Export Control Committee and hiring a new chief compliance officer. Separately, President Trump posted that the attorney general has no evidence Huang engaged in any unlawful conduct.

The market reacted fast. Nvidia stock closed Friday at about one hundred twenty-five dollars, down twenty-eight percent from its November eleventh close, in what one outlet called its worst week since October two thousand eight. In premarket trading Monday it was back to about one hundred fifty-nine dollars, up roughly twenty-seven to thirty percent, though still well below its October record.

Next, a story about how far ahead the frontier labs think they are. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published the internal scores the company has used to track the raw intelligence of its models since G.P.T. three point five. The metric is log-perplexity, which measures how well a model predicts the next token. Lower is better. Pachocki tied OpenAI's biggest new skills in cybersecurity and math to scores below zero point eight, down from about zero point nine nine for the model behind the original ChatGPT. OpenAI also revealed an unreleased internal model at around zero point seven three, and Pachocki says to "expect further transformative capabilities" in the zero point six eight to zero point seven range. He is asking rival labs to agree on shared limits until tools for monitoring A.I. catch up, and Sam Altman called the post "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while."

That fits with other research in today's newsletters. TLDR highlights a METR report finding that the length of tasks frontier models can complete doubles about every three point six months, and that public evidence can't rule out a drastic speedup in A.I. research within the next couple of years. A counterpoint piece argues the most influential A.I. forecasts are wrong, and that real-world capability keeps falling short of what they predict. And a short essay warns that penalizing detected reward hacking during training could teach models to evade monitors.

OpenAI also says it is stepping up defenses against distillation, where outsiders train cheaper copies of a model on its outputs, citing past attempts it tied to DeepSeek and Moonshot, the maker of Kimi.

Now, a security story worth your attention. Between July and November, the security firm Dragos seeded about two million prompts containing unique credentials and web links, pushed them through A.I. inference providers, and watched to see what got used. Three providers were breached, the earliest from mid-July. Some of the stolen requests were flagged as zero data retention, the setting that is supposed to mean a prompt is never stored. On Sunday, OpenRouter removed three providers, stripped one hundred twenty-five endpoints of zero-retention eligibility, and notified affected customers, saying its own systems were not breached. Phala says an attacker had server access and could view request data, and Together A.I. says a party downloaded stored prompt samples after a credential was committed to GitHub. Dragos's H.D. Moore put it plainly: "A privacy flag doesn't help when the attacker is already inside."

On the model front, the weekend's notable open-weights release comes from Mira Murati's Thinking Machines. Inkling 2 Mini has twenty-six point four billion total parameters with three point four billion active per token, a one million token context, and runs on a Mac with thirty-two gigabytes of memory. Its unusual design runs its middle layers three times per token, so it gets more computation without writing more reasoning down. The lab reports it leads open-weight models under one hundred billion parameters on the DeepSWE coding benchmark, and Artificial Analysis says it ties Qwen three point eight at a fraction of the cost and time. Thinking Machines itself flags that the looping is harder to monitor.

Also in open models: Tencent released Hy4, a multimodal model with a seven hundred seventy billion parameter backbone and million-token context. Nvidia released Nemotron Labs Sigma, a family of small diffusion language models that generate text by denoising rather than one token at a time, with a reported three and a half times speedup. And Hugging Face introduced harness adapters to tune models for specific agent setups.

A few quick hits to close. Amazon is reportedly nearing a seven point five billion dollar deal to buy Decart, a three-year-old A.I. startup. SoftBank's Masayoshi Son reportedly floated a one trillion dollar takeover of SK Hynix, though SoftBank now says the deal is off the table. Anthropic closed its I.P.O. with the full underwriter option exercised, about one hundred nineteen point five billion dollars gross including a private placement. Treasury Secretary Bessent says Trump and Xi agreed A.I. should not be allowed to be autonomous for major military actions, while Trump separately teased penalties on U.S. companies that open data centers abroad. Google detailed Agent Compute Engine, which keeps credentials out of an agent's workspace entirely. OpenAI is testing its always-on dots agents with some ChatGPT Plus users, and its terms now hold users responsible for what those agents do on their behalf. Signal now restricts screenshots by default to keep A.I. agents out of private chats. And Gemini can now make short GIFs.

That's the digest for today.
