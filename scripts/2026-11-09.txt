Good morning. This is your A.I. news digest for Monday, November ninth. Today's episode draws on The Rundown, TLDR A.I., and A.I. Breakfast. Three big threads today: a scandal around Nvidia's C.E.O., OpenAI's smaller than hoped fundraise, and more on just how fast A.I. may be accelerating A.I. research.

We start with Nvidia. On Sunday, Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into reports that C.E.O. Jensen Huang "has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort." A committee of independent directors is running the review with outside counsel. The board says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised, that Huang is cooperating, and that it retains confidence in him. He stays on as C.E.O. while the review runs.

In a memo to staff, Huang admitted to an extramarital affair, writing, "I was unfaithful to my wife," and "There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." The relationship was with Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, no relation, a senior executive at the Singapore data center firm Megaspeed and an Nvidia customer. U.S. officials reportedly believe China's Ministry of State Security recruited her to push Huang toward lobbying Washington for looser export limits on advanced chips, with surveillance reportedly tracing the relationship back to at least June twenty twenty-four. The New York Times reports that Secretary of State Marco Rubio oversaw a classified memo warning officials about it last spring. A.I. Breakfast stresses that the intelligence claims should be treated as reported suspicion, not established fact. China denies the spying claims, and reporting has yet to establish what, if anything, she learned from him. Notably, just last month Huang had described their meetings as completely normal.

The political pressure is building. House Republicans asked for a closed-door briefing, and Huang has until November eleventh to respond; he has since been called to testify, and more congressional investigations are expected. President Trump called Huang "an honorable guy" and backed him staying in the job.

The market backdrop is rough. Nvidia went into the weekend down about eight point eight percent for the week, its fifth straight losing week, and roughly one point two seven trillion dollars below its October sixth peak. At A.I. Day Seoul this morning, Nvidia declined to say whether congressional pressure could affect its partnerships with Samsung and S.K. Hynix or its G.P.U. deliveries to Korea, and Korean outlets say the controversy has the country worried about U.S. chip curbs. Separately, Nvidia, S.K. Telecom, and Rebellions signed an agreement to put Korean N.P.U. chips into Nvidia racks. And The Verge reports that two Chinese firms cleared to buy restricted Nvidia H two hundred chips appear to operate out of empty offices.

Next, OpenAI's money. OpenAI reportedly closed an eleven billion dollar first tranche on Friday at a one point two five trillion dollar valuation, according to Bloomberg, up from eight hundred fifty-two billion in March. The round may reach twenty billion dollars over the coming weeks. But it was originally shopped at around thirty billion dollars or more, at a one point four trillion dollar valuation, and was cut back as A.I. stocks sold off. No investors were named and OpenAI did not comment. A.I. Breakfast's take: this isn't a failure to raise, it's OpenAI discovering the current clearing price, and it's the first time in two years that its number moved down on the way through.

Anthropic is feeling the same market. Its I.P.O. roadshow was targeted for the week of November sixteenth, but unnamed investors told the Wall Street Journal the one point five trillion dollar target may need to come down toward one trillion, with one floating an outright postponement. No price range has been set. More broadly, A.I. companies have priced more than three hundred billion dollars of stock and convertibles this year, with another hundred billion queued up. And Thinking Machines Lab is reportedly raising a five billion dollar round led by Accel at a forty billion dollar valuation.

Anthropic also made news on power. The Financial Times reports Anthropic signed a three point four billion dollar deal in late September for one point seven gigawatts of large gas-fired generators. The twist: San Antonio power supplier OnePWR had ordered them from Caterpillar about two years ago and needed money to complete the purchase. Anthropic, which had planned to buy power from OnePWR, instead took over the orders, the place in line, and the installation work. Both companies declined to comment. Since generator lead times are the binding constraint on new capacity, buying a two-year-old spot in the queue beats ordering your own. The timing is awkward, though: voters went against data centers in forty of forty-one ballot measures last Tuesday, and gas generators next to data centers are exactly what those measures were about.

Now to A.I. accelerating A.I. research. TLDR A.I. highlights an essay arguing that A.I. is dramatically accelerating A.I. research right now. Anthropic claims it may imminently meet its own threshold of "dramatically accelerated progress due to A.I.," and some researchers believe it has already crossed it. The essay calls this possibly the biggest A.I. risk humanity has faced, and says the industry isn't prepared to handle it safely.

That ties to the METR assessment we covered Friday, and A.I. Breakfast adds more detail. METR found Anthropic's internal capabilities index growing at about one point nine times its earlier rate, with roughly eighty percent of that speedup attributed to A.I. doing the work, since compute scaling didn't accelerate. But Anthropic held redaction rights and, by METR's account, cut a majority of the evidence METR found most informative. A planned check of how compute is split between capabilities and safety was canceled midway by Anthropic. And if you measure against every developer's models rather than Anthropic's alone, the acceleration estimate drops to one point four times. On the other side of the ledger, a thread on OpenAI's own report about automating its research says models still struggle with its R and D work, with reliability low.

A striking data point on what these systems can do: Physical Superintelligence, an A.I. for physics lab, says its system, running an unreleased Anthropic model, proved the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, a key open problem in quantum complexity theory since twenty oh eight. The result shows quantum computers are not exponentially faster than classical ones on random inputs. Stanford researchers called it "a huge result."

Anthropic also published research on agent memory, including a process it calls "dreaming," where Claude agents use idle time to check their saved notes against logs of past work and reorganize them. Anthropic says newer models have gotten much better at judging what to save, and that explaining why memory matters works better than rules about what to write. In Pokémon tests, Fable five earned four badges with only ten memory files, an older Sonnet three point seven earned one using four hundred thirteen, and Opus five point five beat the game. Anthropic also wrote about Claude playing the online games EVE Online and EVE Frontier.

On policy: in a post-midterm interview, President Trump said he doesn't "mind guardrails" on A.I., as long as the U.S. isn't left in a "deep second position." And the Associated Press reports staff at the Commerce Department's A.I. safety center were asked to report informally to a White House office.

On jobs, Fortune reports nineteen point one percent of U.S. workers replaced by A.I. say their old employer asked them back, and half wanted a raise. U.S. professional and business services added seventy thousand jobs in October, the biggest gain since July twenty twenty-two.
